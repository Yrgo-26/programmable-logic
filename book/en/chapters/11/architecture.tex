\section{The controller's architecture}
\label{c11:sec:arch}

Every rule from \chapref{c10:ch} maps onto one of five blocks:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{\code{bit_timer}} owns nothing about \emph{what} is sent, only \emph{when}. It is the
    only thing in this design that even borders on a clock domain crossing: it free-runs continuously
    once enabled, and resynchronises exactly once per frame, at SOF (\secref{c10:sec:stuffing}'s
    clock recovery argument, applied).
  \item \textbf{\code{tx_shift_reg}} and \textbf{\code{rx_shift_reg}} own the bit stuffing and
    nothing else: they serialise or deserialise up to 8 real bits at a time and transparently insert
    or remove stuff bits. They have no idea whether the bits they shift are an ID, a data byte or a
    CRC value; that is \code{can_controller}'s job.
  \item \textbf{\code{crc15}} owns the CRC-15 computation, one bit at a time, with no notion of
    ``frames'' or ``fields'' either; it merely accumulates the bit it is given, when it is told to.
  \item \textbf{\code{can_controller}} is the only block that knows the actual CAN frame format: it
    sequences through SOF, arbitration, the control field, data, CRC, ACK and EOF, in order, drives
    the other four blocks and reads their outputs. Detecting lost arbitration also lives here, since
    it is specifically about comparing what this controller decided to send against what the bus
    actually shows.
\end{itemize}

\lecfig[0.92]{lectures/L11/appendix/images/can_controller_diagram.png}{\code{can_controller}'s block
architecture.}{c11:fig:arch}

Two conventions keep the picture readable. \code{clock} reaches every subblock, and the synchronised
\code{reset_s2_n} reaches all but the two \code{meta_prev} instances producing it and its bus
counterpart; both are left undrawn, as is usual in a block diagram. Every output port
(\code{tx_done}, \code{tx_bus}, \code{bus_en}, \code{rx_id}, \code{rx_dlc}, \code{rx_data},
\code{rx_valid}, \code{error}) is driven by the frame state machine and by nothing else.

Three of the eleven internal connections are worth reading twice, since they are the ones people
redraw wrongly from memory:
\begin{itemize}
  \item The two lines at \code{crc15} run in \textbf{opposite} directions. \code{clear},
    \code{enable} and \code{data} go up into it, one real bit at a time; \code{crc} and \code{valid}
    come back down out of it. (\code{clear} is \chapref{c13:ch}'s per-frame reset \textemdash{} the
    engine has to start every frame at zero, and an aborted frame would otherwise leave residue
    behind; \chapref{c16:ch} drives it from \code{STATE_START}.)
  \item \code{rx_shift_reg} has \textbf{three} lines arriving: \code{sample} from \code{bit_timer},
    \code{rx_bus_s2} from the synchroniser \chapref{c12:ch} adds, and \code{bit_count} plus
    \code{enable} from the frame state machine. Without the first two it has no data source at all.
  \item \code{tx_shift_reg} and \code{rx_shift_reg} are never connected to each other, and neither is
    connected to \code{crc15}. The frame state machine carries every bit between them.
    \code{rx_shift_reg} is likewise the only subblock that reads the bus at all, and even that reads
    the synchronised copy rather than the port; see \secref{c11:sec:sync}.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{How they nest}
The diagram reads as a layer picture, with the cheapest concern on top, but the actual VHDL hierarchy
is containment, not a pipeline: \code{can_controller} instantiates all four of \code{bit_timer},
\code{crc15}, \code{tx_shift_reg} and \code{rx_shift_reg} inside itself, alongside the
\code{meta_prev} synchroniser \chapref{c12:ch} adds. \code{can_controller} is therefore the only
entity you ever instantiate by hand, and the whole controller is one component from outside, exactly
as the register bank in \chapref{c18:ch} will see it.

Two more layers then sit above it: \code{register_bank} (\chapref{c18:ch}), turning those
register-level ports into the register map a driver polls, and \code{can_spi_node}
(\chapref{c19:ch}), the top level adding the SPI transport and the board's pins. Both belong to
reaching the controller, not to speaking CAN, which is why they turn up in the last two chapters
rather than here.

This split \textemdash{} one block per \emph{responsibility}, not one block per \emph{frame field}
\textemdash{} is deliberate: \code{tx_shift_reg} is reused for the ID, the control field, every data
byte and the CRC value, simply reloaded with different data and a different bit count each time,
instead of writing nearly identical shift logic five separate times.

\section{Following a frame through the blocks}
\label{c11:sec:trace}

Naming the five blocks is not the same as knowing how they cooperate. This section takes a concrete
frame, ID \code{0x123}, DLC \code{2}, data bytes \code{A1 B2}, and follows it through the
architecture above. There is no VHDL here; the question is only which block does what, and what
passes between them.

\paragraph{First, the only block that counts time}
\code{bit_timer} divides the 50 MHz clock into bit periods and emits two pulses per period:
\code{sample}, 70~\% into the period, once the bus level has had time to settle, and
\code{bit_done}, at the very end, when the current bit is over. Every other block acts on one of the
two pulses, and none of them counts clock cycles against the bus itself. Transmission moves on
\code{bit_done}, at the boundary where a new bit begins. Reception moves on \code{sample}, in the
middle of a bit already sitting there. That difference in particular is why a receiver reads the
value a transmitter put out, instead of catching it mid-change.

\paragraph{Transmission: 60 bits, 50 of them through a register}
With the field widths from \chapref{c10:ch}, this frame is $1$ (SOF) $+\ 11$ (ID) $+\ 1$ (RTR)
$+\ 2$ (IDE, r0) $+\ 4$ (DLC) $+\ 16$ (data) $+\ 15$ (CRC) $=$ \textbf{50 bits in the stuffed
region}, followed by an unstuffed tail of $1 + 1 + 1 + 7 =$ \textbf{10 bits}. Sixty bits before any
stuff bit is inserted.

Those 50 bits reach the bus as \textbf{eight loads of a single \code{tx_shift_reg}}:

\begin{narrowtable}{clr}
  \thead{\#} & \thead{Group} & \thead{Bits} \\
  \midrule
  1 & SOF & 1 \\
  2 & Identifier, high part & 8 \\
  3 & Identifier, low part + RTR & 4 \\
  4 & IDE, r0, DLC & 6 \\
  5 & Data byte 0 (\code{A1}) & 8 \\
  6 & Data byte 1 (\code{B2}) & 8 \\
  7 & CRC, high part & 8 \\
  8 & CRC, low part & 7 \\
\end{narrowtable}

\code{can_controller} reloads the same 8-bit register eight times, each time with a different value
and a different bit count. That is the ``one block per responsibility'' claim above, made concrete:
the identifier is no field to the shift register, it is group 2. The remaining 10 tail bits never
reach a shift register at all; they are fixed recessive levels, and \code{can_controller} drives them
directly while counting \code{bit_done} pulses.

Within a group, four things happen per bit:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \code{tx_shift_reg} produces the next bit. The load presents the first one immediately; every
    \code{bit_done} after that shifts out the next. The register also decides, on its own, whether a
    stuff bit should be inserted, and announces on \code{stuff} which kind of bit this was.
  \item \code{can_controller} turns the bit into bus levels. Dominant means \code{bus_en = '1'} with
    \code{tx_bus = '0'}; recessive means \code{bus_en = '0'}, which releases the bus. A node never
    drives recessive actively (\secref{c10:sec:can}'s wired-AND).
  \item \code{crc15} is fed the same bit, but only when it was a real one. Stuff bits are skipped.
    \code{crc15} has no idea what a stuff bit is; being told when \emph{not} to accumulate is its
    entire interface on that side.
  \item \code{can_controller} compares \code{rx_bus} against the bit it has just driven. That
    comparison is the arbitration, and it stays here rather than in a subblock because it needs both
    what this node intended and what the bus actually shows, and \code{can_controller} is the only
    block holding both.
\end{itemize}

After group 6, \code{crc15} holds the CRC over everything sent so far. \code{can_controller} reads it
and feeds it straight back into \code{tx_shift_reg} as groups 7 and 8. Note what that means: the
engine computing the CRC and the register sending it are never connected to each other.
\code{can_controller} carries the value across, which is why neither block has to know that a CRC
field exists.

\paragraph{Reception: the same path, mirrored}
\code{rx_shift_reg} samples \code{rx_bus} at \code{bit_timer.sample}, removes stuff bits and reports
every real bit on \code{real_bit} and \code{real_bit_valid}. Those two signals feed \code{crc15}
exactly as \code{tx_shift_reg}'s do during transmission: the same instance, the same rule about
accumulating real bits only, no mode switch anywhere (which is \chapref{c13:ch}'s point about one
engine doing both jobs). Completed groups arrive on \code{data}, and \code{can_controller} slices
them back into \code{rx_id}, \code{rx_dlc} and \code{rx_data}. A stuff bit that fails to appear
raises \code{stuff_error}, and \code{rx_shift_reg} stops there: it reports the violation without
deciding what it means. Deciding is \code{can_controller}'s job, and it decides to abort the frame
and raise \code{error}.

\paragraph{What the walkthrough shows}
No subblock knows which field it is handling. Each reports facts about \emph{bits}: one is done, that
one was inserted, a stuff bit was missing, this group is complete. \code{can_controller} supplies
every bit of the meaning. Three things follow, and all three are worth having:
\begin{itemize}
  \item The four subblocks can be tested without CAN. Their testbenches in \chapref{c12:ch} to
    \ref{c15:ch} drive bit patterns and check bit patterns; not one of them needs a whole frame to be
    meaningful.
  \item \code{can_controller} is the only block that would change if the frame format did. Extended
    29-bit identifiers, for example, would mean different groups, not different blocks.
  \item Every connection between blocks is a one-bit or one-byte signal with exactly one writer.
    There is no shared bus or common structure two blocks both reach into, which is what keeps the
    wiring work in \chapref{c12:ch} to \ref{c15:ch} to one port map at a time rather than a redesign
    every chapter.
\end{itemize}

\section{The controller's interface}
\label{c11:sec:interface}

\code{can_controller}'s ports are what the outside world sees, and they stay fixed for the rest of
the book: \chapref{c12:ch} to \ref{c17:ch} add subblocks \emph{inside} this entity without changing a
single port. Two things connect to it, and every port belongs to one of them.

\lecfig[0.66]{lectures/L11/appendix/images/can_controller.png}{\code{can_controller}'s fifteen
ports.}{c11:fig:ports}

\paragraph{The bus side, five ports}
These are the ports reaching the outside world as physical pins; the first three face the CAN
transceiver:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \code{rx_bus} is the current bus level, one bit, straight from the transceiver and
    asynchronous to \code{clock}. The controller synchronises it itself, with a \code{meta_prev}
    instance \chapref{c12:ch} builds, so that the board's top level can connect this port to a pin
    and think no more about it. The controller reads it to receive, and also to monitor its own
    transmissions during arbitration.
  \item \code{tx_bus} is the bit this node wants to drive, and \code{bus_en} says whether it is
    driving at all. The pair models open-drain behaviour: \code{bus_en = '1'} with
    \code{tx_bus = '0'} pulls the bus dominant, and \code{bus_en = '0'} releases it so that another
    node can. A node never drives recessive actively; it simply lets go. That is what makes
    \secref{c10:sec:can}'s wired-AND work.
  \item \code{clock} is the 50 MHz system clock, and \code{reset_n} a \textbf{raw}, active-low reset,
    straight from a pin. Like \code{rx_bus} it is asynchronous, and like \code{rx_bus} the controller
    synchronises it itself. The synchronised copy is an internal signal called \code{reset_s2_n}, the
    name the \code{reset_sync} exercise gave it in \chapref{c4:ch} and the name every subblock in the
    project still takes.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{The transmit side, five ports}
These are the ones a caller fills in to send a frame:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \code{tx_id}, \code{tx_dlc} and \code{tx_data} carry the frame itself, typed as \code{id_t},
    \code{dlc_t} and \code{data_t} from \code{can_def}. \code{tx_data} holds byte 0 in its most
    significant bits, in the same order the bus sends them.
  \item \code{tx_req} requests transmission. The controller acts on it only when it is itself idle
    \textbf{and the bus has been idle too}, since a node must not start driving on top of a frame
    already in progress (\chapref{c16:ch}). A request raised while the bus is busy is discarded
    rather than queued, so the caller has to ask again.
  \item \code{tx_done} pulses high for one cycle as soon as this node's own frame has finished
    sending.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{The receive side, five ports}
They mirror the transmit side exactly:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \code{rx_id}, \code{rx_dlc} and \code{rx_data} hold the most recently received frame, the
    same types and the same byte layout as their \code{tx_} counterparts.
  \item \code{rx_valid} pulses high for one cycle when a complete, valid frame has arrived.
  \item \code{error} is the only port that is \emph{not} a pulse: it goes high on lost arbitration, a
    stuffing violation, a failed CRC check or a received remote frame (a recessive RTR bit), and
    stays high until the next frame begins. A caller polling it now and then still sees the error.
\end{itemize}

Four things about this interface are worth noticing now, since they shape everything that follows:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{There is no serialisation in it.} The caller hands over an identifier, a length and
    bytes. Every bit of the framing, the stuffing and the CRC that \chapref{c10:ch} described happens
    inside, on the way to \code{tx_bus}. That is the whole point of building a controller.
  \item \textbf{Transmission and reception are symmetric but not simultaneous.} A node either sends
    its own frame or receives somebody else's, never both, so the interface's two halves are never
    active at the same time.
  \item \textbf{\code{tx_done}, \code{rx_valid} and \code{error} are the whole status interface.}
    Every status bit the register map offers a driver (\chapref{c18:ch}) is derived from one of the
    three.
  \item \textbf{Every port on the register side is assumed synchronous to \code{clock}.}
    \code{rx_bus} is the exception, and the controller handles that itself with \code{meta_prev}. The
    rest, the transmit ports a caller drives and the status and receive ports it reads, assume a
    caller in the same clock domain, which is exactly what \code{register_bank} (\chapref{c18:ch})
    is. A caller in a \emph{different} domain needs a handshake in front of them, not a wider
    \code{meta_prev}: running 64 bits of \code{tx_data} through a bit-by-bit synchroniser removes the
    metastability but still lets the controller latch a value that was never written, the same skew
    problem \chapref{c19:ch} sets out for the switch bank. A synchronisation chain is the right tool
    for one bit whose neighbours are independent, and the wrong tool for a value whose bits have to
    agree.
\end{itemize}

\section{Writing \code{can_controller.vhd}}
\label{c11:sec:writing}

This chapter writes the file from scratch, to \file{controller/can_controller.vhd}, beside the
\file{can_def.vhd} written in \chapref{c10:ch}.

\textbf{The entity} declares the fifteen ports described above, in exactly this order, which is what
\code{can_controller_tb} binds to:

\begin{textdrawing}
 1 clock       4 tx_id     7 rx_bus     10 bus_en    13 rx_data
 2 reset_n     5 tx_dlc    8 tx_done    11 rx_id     14 rx_valid
 3 tx_req      6 tx_data   9 tx_bus     12 rx_dlc    15 error
\end{textdrawing}

\textbf{Every input first, then every output}, which is this book's convention everywhere and is why
\code{rx_bus} sits seventh rather than beside the bus outputs it conceptually belongs with. Read the
block diagram above straight down its left column and then its right, and you get 1 to 15 in order.
The types come from \code{can_def}, so the file opens with \code{use work.can_def.all;} alongside the
usual \code{ieee.std_logic_1164}, and the port list mixes plain \code{std_logic} with the package's
subtypes. The first lines set the pattern for all fifteen:

\begin{vhdlcode}
library ieee;
use ieee.std_logic_1164.all;
use work.can_def.all;

entity can_controller is
    port(clock   : in std_logic;
         reset_n : in std_logic;
         tx_req  : in std_logic;
         tx_id   : in id_t;
         ...
\end{vhdlcode}

\begin{warning}
Get the \textbf{order} exactly right, since it is what everything binds to: \code{can_controller_tb}
associates its ports positionally, and so does every instantiation in this book. The names are yours.
A swapped pair analyses perfectly well today, and unlike a misspelled name it will not fail to
analyse in \chapref{c17:ch} either. It will silently connect the wrong signals together, and the
testbench will report behaviour that does not add up, six chapters later.
\end{warning}

\textbf{The architecture starts empty.} Not a placeholder to be replaced later, but genuinely empty:

\begin{vhdlcode}
architecture behaviour of can_controller is
begin
end architecture;
\end{vhdlcode}

That analyses and elaborates cleanly, and \code{make build} confirms that it does. Write it, check
it, and look at it for a moment: an entity with no behaviour at all is still a complete design unit.
Then the rest of the project puts the first thing inside it.

\paragraph{Why write it now, empty?}
Because the interface is a decision, and it is one this chapter is in a position to make. You know
what a CAN frame contains (\chapref{c10:ch}), you know which blocks will do the work
(\secref{c11:sec:arch}), and you know what a driver eventually needs to read and write
(\secref{c11:sec:regmap}). All that \chapref{c12:ch} onwards adds is \emph{behaviour behind ports
that already exist}.

The alternative, writing the subblocks first and discovering the top level's interface at the end, is
how the design would emerge if nobody had thought about it in advance. It also means that the shape
of what you are building stays a mystery right up until the very last chapter.

\paragraph{What each chapter adds}
From here on every chapter builds one block and instantiates it here, so that the architecture fills
in from the inside. \Chapref{c12:ch} does two, since both are small:

\begin{booktable}{@{}p{6em}L@{}}
  \thead{Chapter} & \thead{Adds to \code{can_controller}} \\
  \midrule
  \ref{c12:ch} & \code{meta_prev} twice, synchronising \code{reset_n} and \code{rx_bus}, then
    \code{bit_timer} \\
  \ref{c13:ch} & \code{crc15}, and the signals feeding it one bit at a time \\
  \ref{c14:ch} & \code{tx_shift_reg} \\
  \ref{c15:ch} & \code{rx_shift_reg} \\
  \ref{c16:ch} & The frame-sequencing state machine, the transmit path \\
  \ref{c17:ch} & The receive path and the arbitration, completing the design \\
\end{booktable}

The file has to analyse cleanly at every one of those steps, not just at the end. \code{make build}
checks every module separately as soon as it exists, so an entity that does not compile is caught in
the chapter that wrote it.

\section{What the controller synchronises, and why that is the controller's job}
\label{c11:sec:sync}

One decision follows directly from the last point in \secref{c11:sec:interface}, and it is worth
making explicit here since every later chapter depends on it. \code{rx_bus} arrives from a
transceiver on another chip, and \code{reset_n} from a button or a supervisor, so nothing in this
FPGA has ever controlled either of them. Sampling either straight into \code{clock}'s domain can
leave a flip-flop metastable, the problem \chapref{c4:ch} solved with two flip-flops in series.

Those two are the controller's \emph{only} asynchronous inputs, and they are asynchronous whoever
instantiates it, so \textbf{the controller synchronises them itself} rather than leaving it to the
board's top level. Whoever integrates it connects both straight to pins and thinks no more about it.
Everything else on the entity is the register interface, which is a different contract: one a caller
in this clock domain satisfies for free, and one a caller in another domain has to satisfy with a
handshake rather than a wider synchroniser.

Internally the synchronised copies are called \code{reset_s2_n} and \code{rx_bus_s2}, following
\chapref{c4:ch}'s convention from \code{reset_sync}, and those are the names every subblock from
\chapref{c12:ch} onwards takes. \textbf{No block ever reads \code{reset_n} or \code{rx_bus}
directly}, the arbitration comparison in \chapref{c17:ch} included. The module producing them,
\code{meta_prev}, is \chapref{c12:ch}'s, alongside the bit timer; this chapter establishes that the
controller owns the problem, and the next builds what solves it.

\section{A first look at the register map}
\label{c11:sec:regmap}

A parallel class writes a C++ driver against this controller, and talks to it through
\textbf{registers accessed over SPI} rather than via VHDL ports directly. You build the layers
presenting those registers: \code{register_bank} in \chapref{c18:ch} and \code{spi_reg_bridge} in
\chapref{c19:ch}. The whole map and the transaction format are in appendix~\ref{app:protocol}, and
together they are the entire contract between the two classes.

It is worth a preview here, seven chapters in advance, because it is exactly the same information
\code{can_controller}'s ports already carry \textemdash{} the later layers change the information's
\emph{shape}, never its content:

\begin{booktable}{@{}p{14em}L@{}}
  \thead{Concept (\code{can_controller})} & \thead{Register in the map} \\
  \midrule
  \code{tx_id}, \code{tx_dlc}, \code{tx_data} & \code{TX_ID}, \code{TX_DLC},
    \code{TX_DATA_LO}/\code{HI} \\
  \code{tx_req}, \code{tx_done} & \code{TX_SEND}, one bit in \code{STATUS} \\
  \code{rx_id}, \code{rx_dlc}, \code{rx_data} & \code{RX_ID}, \code{RX_DLC},
    \code{RX_DATA_LO}/\code{HI} \\
  \code{rx_valid} & one bit in \code{STATUS}, cleared via \code{RX_ACK} \\
  \code{error} & \code{ERROR_FLAGS} \\
\end{booktable}

Keep this correspondence in mind through \chapref{c19:ch}: everything the parallel class's driver
reads or writes is just this controller's own port-level interface, wrapped in registers.
