\section{Interface}
\label{c14:sec:iface}

\modulefig[0.62]{lectures/L14/appendix/images/tx_shift_reg.png}

\begin{booktable}{@{}p{5.6em}p{3.4em}p{10em}L@{}}
  \thead{Port} & \thead{Dir.} & \thead{Type} & \thead{Meaning} \\
  \midrule
  \code{clock}, \code{reset_s2_n} & in & \code{std_logic} & 50 MHz clock, synchronised active-low
    reset. \\
  \code{load} & in & \code{std_logic} & Latches \code{data}/\code{bit_count} as the next group
    \textbf{and} presents its first bit this cycle. \\
  \code{data} & in & \code{byte_t} & The group, with its \code{bit_count} valid bits packed into the
    \textbf{most} significant bits (the first bit sent is bit 7). \\
  \code{bit_count} & in & \code{std_logic_vector(3 downto 0)} & Number of valid bits in \code{data},
    1--8. \\
  \code{shift} & in & \code{std_logic} & Pulses once per CAN bit (from \code{bit_timer.bit_done}) to
    drive the stream forward. \\
  \code{tx_bit} & out & \code{std_logic} & Registered bit to drive during the coming bit period. \\
  \code{stuff} & out & \code{std_logic} & \code{'1'} on the very shift that puts out a stuff bit;
    masks that bit out of the CRC feed. \\
  \code{done} & out & \code{std_logic} & Pulses when the group has been fully sent (see the
    timing). \\
  \code{bit_valid} & out & \code{std_logic} & \code{'1'} the one cycle where a new bit was put onto
    \code{tx_bit}. \\
\end{booktable}

\code{tx_bit} is the only output that is a \emph{level}, held until the next bit is presented.
\code{stuff}, \code{done} and \code{bit_valid} are all \textbf{one-cycle pulses}: default them to
\code{'0'} at the top of the clocked process and raise them only in the branch that earns them, the
same idiom \code{bit_timer} uses for \code{sample} and \code{bit_done}.

That matters most for \code{bit_valid} and \code{stuff}, since \code{can_controller} builds
\code{crc15}'s enable out of the pair, as \code{bit_valid and not stuff} (\chapref{c16:ch}):
\code{bit_valid} says a bit was put out, and \code{stuff} masks out those put-out bits the CRC must
never see, since the CRC covers real bits only. Hold \code{bit_valid} as a level instead and the
engine integrates the same bit at every clock edge of the bit period, fifty times over, silently
destroying the CRC.

\code{load} and \code{shift} are mutually exclusive within a cycle; \code{can_controller} never
asserts both. \code{tx_shift_reg_tb} binds \textbf{positionally}, so the order and the types above are
what has to match; the names are yours.

\section{What the module has to remember}
\label{c14:sec:state}

Start where the introduction pointed: the paper version of the bit stuffing rule looked \emph{back
over the sequence}, and the hardware has no sequence to look back over. So before any process is
written, list what has to live in a register. Everything below follows from the module's two jobs
\textemdash{} keeping a group moving, and keeping the bit stuffing rule honest \textemdash{} and
every rule in the rest of this chapter speaks in terms of exactly these five signals. The names are
the ones this chapter uses; keeping them makes the text and your code talk about the same thing.

\begin{booktable}{@{}p{7em}>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{9.6em}L@{}}
  \thead{Signal} & \thead{Type} & \thead{Holds} \\
  \midrule
  \code{shift_reg} & \code{byte_t} & The group's remaining bits, with the next bit in bit 7. \\
  \code{bits_left} & integer, 0 to 8 & Number of real bits in the group not yet presented. \\
  \code{last_bit} & \code{std_logic} & The bit most recently placed on the wire. \\
  \code{consecutive} & integer, 0 to \code{MAX_RUN} & The length of the current run; 0 means none. \\
  \code{stuff_pending} & \code{std_logic} & A stuff bit is owed at the next shift. \\
\end{booktable}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{\code{shift_reg} and \code{bits_left} are the group in flight.} The register holds
    the unsent bits left-aligned so that ``the next bit'' is always \code{shift_reg(7)};
    \code{bits_left} is how many real bits remain. There is no more group bookkeeping than that.
  \item \textbf{\code{last_bit} and \code{consecutive} are the bit stuffing rule's memory}, and
    together they are called the \textbf{run tracker} below: the bit most recently sent, and how many
    identical bits in a row it ended. \code{MAX_RUN} identical bits \textemdash{} five, a constant
    \code{can_def} supplies, since both ends of the link have to agree on it exactly \textemdash{} is
    a full run; the pair is everything the rule ever needed from ``looking back''. Both are written
    in exactly one place, a procedure the process declares for the purpose (below).
  \item \textbf{\code{stuff_pending} is the rule's one-bit debt.} When a run reaches five, the stuff
    bit is \emph{not} sent immediately \textemdash{} the fifth bit still needs its own full period on
    the bus. Reaching five therefore only \emph{arms} an insertion, and this flag carries the debt
    forward to the next \code{shift}.
\end{itemize}

The outputs are registered in the same process: \code{tx_bit} is a level, held until the next bit
replaces it; \code{stuff}, \code{done} and \code{bit_valid} are the one-cycle pulses
\secref{c14:sec:iface} described, defaulted low at the top of the process and raised only in the
branch that earns them.

\section{The process, top to bottom}
\label{c14:sec:process}

One clocked process holds all of the above: asynchronous reset first, and on the rising edge first the
defaults and then either the load branch or the shift branch. The rules below follow that order. The
chapter says what each branch has to do; how that is expressed in VHDL is yours.

\subsection{The run tracker, written once}
\label{c14:sub:tracker}

Every branch putting out a bit needs the same few rules, so they live in a procedure,
\code{track_run}, with the placed bit as its only parameter, declared in the process's own declarative
part, between \code{process(...) is} and \code{begin}. For the placed bit:
\begin{itemize}
  \item If it equals \code{last_bit} and \code{consecutive} is not 0, the run continues: count
    \code{consecutive} up, and if the run thereby reaches \code{MAX_RUN}, arm \code{stuff_pending}.
  \item Otherwise a new run begins: \code{consecutive} becomes 1.
  \item In both cases \code{last_bit} becomes the placed bit.
\end{itemize}

This is the book's first subprogram, so four things about it before anything calls it:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{It reaches the signals directly.} \code{last_bit}, \code{consecutive} and
    \code{stuff_pending} are architecture signals, visible inside the procedure because the procedure
    is declared inside the process, which sits inside the architecture. They are not parameters and
    should not be; only the \emph{placed bit} differs between callers, and that is the only parameter
    there is.
  \item \textbf{Declaring it inside the process is what makes the signal assignments legal at all.} A
    subprogram may assign a signal only in two situations: the signal is a formal parameter of class
    \code{signal}, or the subprogram is declared inside a process, in which case its assignments use
    that process's drivers, exactly as if the lines had been written at every call site. This
    procedure rests on the second case. Move it up unchanged to the architecture's declarative part
    and it stops compiling, one error per assignment:
    \code{signal "consecutive" is not a formal parameter}. Threading \code{consecutive},
    \code{last_bit} and \code{stuff_pending} through as formal parameters of class \code{signal}
    would satisfy the rule, at the cost of three extra arguments at every call and a real question
    about which process owns the drivers. Declared here, only this process can call it, so only this
    process drives them.
  \item \textbf{The timing is unchanged.} An \code{in} parameter is evaluated at the call, so the
    placed bit carries the pre-edge value the caller passed in, and the signal reads inside the body
    are also from before the edge (the variables-versus-signals rule from \chapref{c5:ch}). The
    procedure behaves exactly like the same lines written inline.
  \item \textbf{Synthesis inlines it.} The result is the same logic replicated under every caller's
    branch condition; nothing is shared, multiplexed or called at run time. This is factoring at the
    source level, and the netlist comes out identical either way.
\end{itemize}

Two details in the rules, and one about where the procedure is called:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{``The run reaches \code{MAX_RUN}'' means that \code{consecutive} reads
    \code{MAX_RUN - 1}.} The read inside the procedure gives the pre-edge value, that is, the run
    length \emph{before} this bit extended it. That semantics is exactly right here, and it is why
    the comparison is made against \code{MAX_RUN - 1} and not \code{MAX_RUN}.
  \item \textbf{Keep the three rules apart.} The restart at 1 belongs to the case where the run is
    \emph{broken}, not to the case where it reaches \code{MAX_RUN}, and \code{last_bit} updates at
    \emph{every} placement, identical or not. Tie either of them to the check against \code{MAX_RUN}
    and a broken run never restarts, or \code{last_bit} freezes.
  \item \textbf{Call it from a branch that places a bit, never from the process's top level.} Nothing
    on the wire changes during the forty-nine-odd clock edges between presentations, so a call
    running at every edge counts phantom bits. It knows nothing about groups either way: the wire has
    no idea where one group ends and the next begins, so neither does the tracker.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Reset and defaults}
\label{c14:sub:defaults}

\textbf{Reset clears everything}: the outputs, the group (\code{shift_reg}, \code{bits_left}) and the
run tracker (\code{last_bit}, \code{consecutive}, \code{stuff_pending}). This is the \textbf{only}
place the tracker is ever cleared. \code{load} must not touch it: the stuffing sees the transmitted
stream as continuous across the whole frame, so a run ending one group and continuing into the next
still forces a stuff bit right at the boundary. (That is the chained-reload scenario, test 3 in the
testbench: one group ends with four \code{'1'}s, the next opens with a \code{'1'}, and the stuff bit
lands before that group's second real bit.)

\textbf{The defaults next, and only these three.} \code{stuff}, \code{done} and \code{bit_valid} are
set to \code{'0'} first thing at every rising edge, the \code{bit_timer} idiom, so the branches below
only \emph{raise} them.

\begin{warning}
\code{stuff_pending} does \textbf{not} belong among them, however much it resembles them. It is
state, not a pulse: armed at the shift completing a run, paid at the \emph{next} shift, a whole bit
period (fifty clock edges) later. Default it low and the debt evaporates at the first idle edge in
between, and the module then simply never stuffs anything. It is written in exactly three places:
cleared at reset, armed by the run tracker, and cleared by the branch that pays it.
\end{warning}

\subsection{The load branch}
\label{c14:sub:load}

\textbf{\code{load = '1'} presents the first bit immediately.} A bit period begins the moment the
previous group's last \code{bit_done} goes, which is also when the next group's \code{load} goes. If
\code{load} merely latched the data and waited for the first \code{shift} pulse, the first period
would still show the previous group's last bit, a whole bit late. So when \code{load = '1'}:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \code{tx_bit} becomes \code{data(7)}: the first bit is put out now.
  \item \code{shift_reg} becomes \code{data} shifted one step left, with a \code{'0'} shifted in at
    the bottom, so that the group's second bit stands in bit 7.
  \item \code{bits_left} becomes \code{bit_count - 1}. The contract says \code{bit_count} is 1 to 8;
    0 would underflow here.
  \item \code{bit_valid} pulses: a new bit is on \code{tx_bit}.
  \item The run tracker runs on \code{data(7)}, the bit just placed on the wire.
\end{itemize}

The last item is neither optional nor a detail: \code{load} places a bit on the wire, so the tracker
has to see it like any other. Leave it out and the run count runs one bit behind for the rest of the
frame; test 1 in the testbench catches it at shift 5, where the stuff bit the five \code{'1'}s earned
fails to appear.

\subsection{The shift branch}
\label{c14:sub:shift}

\textbf{\code{shift = '1'} does exactly one of three things}, never in the same cycle as \code{load},
tested in this priority order:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{A stuff bit is owed} (\code{stuff_pending = '1'}): \code{tx_bit} becomes
    \code{not last_bit}, \code{stuff} and \code{bit_valid} pulse, \code{stuff_pending} is cleared,
    since the debt is paid, and the run tracker runs on the stuff bit. \code{shift_reg} and
    \code{bits_left} are left untouched.
  \item \textbf{The closing shift} (\code{bits_left = 0}): \code{done} pulses, and nothing else
    changes; \code{tx_bit} in particular keeps its value.
  \item \textbf{Otherwise, the next real bit:} the same shape as the load branch, with
    \code{shift_reg} in \code{data}'s place. \code{tx_bit} becomes \code{shift_reg(7)},
    \code{shift_reg} is shifted one step left with a \code{'0'} at the bottom, \code{bits_left}
    counts down, \code{bit_valid} pulses, and the run tracker runs on the placed bit, which may arm a
    stuff bit for the next shift.
\end{enumerate}

A stuff bit goes through the tracker exactly like a real one, and the placed bit being
\code{not last_bit} is what makes that come out right with no special case: it differs from
\code{last_bit} by construction, so the tracker takes the broken-run path and does precisely what the
rule wants, restarting the run at 1 with the stuff bit's own value in \code{last_bit}. Nothing arms a
new debt here either, since the arming belongs to the path that cannot be taken. The group itself
makes no progress.

The closing shift exists so that the last bit gets its full period on the bus: when \code{bits_left}
is 0 every bit has been put out, and this shift marks that the last one has now been out on the bus
for a whole bit period.

The priority order is load-bearing: a pending stuff bit comes before the closing shift, and that is
what makes the special case below work with no extra rule; a group whose \emph{last} real bit
completes a run still gets its stuff bit sent before \code{done} goes. (\code{load} never has an
outstanding stuff bit to worry about: \code{can_controller} loads only after \code{done}, and
\code{done} comes after every pending stuff bit has been sent.)

\section{The outputs' timing, the part the testbench pins down exactly}
\label{c14:sec:timing}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \code{tx_bit} is set by \textbf{both} \code{load} and \code{shift}; the closing shift leaves
    it unchanged.
  \item \code{stuff} pulses only at the shift that actually puts out a stuff bit, never at
    \code{load} (a group's first bit may \emph{trigger} a stuff bit, but that stuff bit is sent at a
    later shift).
  \item \code{done} does \textbf{not} pulse at the shift presenting the group's last real bit or
    stuff bit; that bit first needs its own full period on the bus. It pulses one shift later, at the
    closing shift, which presents nothing new. Letting \code{done} go early would let
    \code{can_controller} load the next group a whole bit period too soon.
  \item \code{bit_valid} is \code{'1'} at \code{load} and at every shift putting out a new bit (real
    or stuff), and \code{'0'} at the closing shift. Consumers passing \code{tx_bit} on bit by bit
    gate on \code{bit_valid}, not on ``load or shift'', or they would sample the closing shift's
    unchanged \code{tx_bit} as a duplicate. \code{crc15} is the consumer that matters, and it needs
    \code{stuff} too: \chapref{c16:ch} gates its enable on \code{bit_valid and not stuff}, since a
    stuff bit is put out on the wire but must never enter the CRC.
  \item Special case: if \code{bit_count = 1} and that single bit triggers a stuff bit, \code{done}
    still waits for the stuff bit to be sent first.
\end{itemize}

\section{One group, presentation by presentation}
\label{c14:sec:trace}

Four timing rules interact here, and prose is a poor way to see them. Below is a complete group:
\code{data = "11111000"}, \code{bit_count = 8}, loaded into a just-reset register. Every row is a
\emph{presentation}, either \code{load} or a \code{shift} pulse, and shows the outputs after it. This
is the same sequence \code{tx_shift_reg_tb} checks in its first test, so your own module has to
reproduce it row for row.

\begin{textdrawing}
         data bit  tx_bit  stuff  bit_valid  done  consecutive
load            1       1      0          1     0            1
shift 1         2       1      0          1     0            2
shift 2         3       1      0          1     0            3
shift 3         4       1      0          1     0            4
shift 4         5       1      0          1     0            5
shift 5         -       0      1          1     0            1
shift 6         6       0      0          1     0            2
shift 7         7       0      0          1     0            3
shift 8         8       0      0          1     0            4
shift 9         -       0      0          0     1            4
\end{textdrawing}

Ten presentations to send eight bits. Each of the four rules shows up in it:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{\code{load} presents immediately.} The first row already has data bit 1 on
    \code{tx_bit}, with \code{bit_valid} high. No \code{shift} has happened yet. Had \code{load}
    merely latched the group, this row would still show the \emph{previous} group's last bit, and the
    whole frame would run one bit period late.
  \item \textbf{The run count drives everything.} It climbs from 1 to 5 over the five \code{'1'}s,
    and at shift 4 it reaches five. That puts out no stuff bit; it \emph{arms} one for the next
    presentation.
  \item \textbf{\code{stuff} is one presentation wide and carries no data.} Shift 5 drives
    \code{not last_bit}, marks \code{stuff = '1'} and resets the run to 1. The data-bit column shows
    \code{-}: the group has made no progress, which is why eight data bits need nine bit-placing
    presentations here. \code{bit_valid} is still \code{'1'}, since a bit really was placed on the
    wire; it just was not one of yours.
  \item \textbf{\code{done} lags by one, and \code{bit_valid} marks the difference.} Shift 8 puts out
    the last real bit, and \code{done} stays low, since that bit has not yet had its bit period on
    the bus. Shift 9 is the closing shift: \code{tx_bit} holds its previous value, nothing new is put
    out, \code{bit_valid} falls to \code{'0'}, and only now does \code{done} pulse.
\end{itemize}

The last row is the one worth staring at. \code{tx_bit} still reads \code{'0'}, exactly as at shift
8, so anything gating on \code{shift} alone would feed \code{crc15} the same bit a second time and
silently corrupt the checksum. \code{bit_valid} is the only output distinguishing ``a bit was placed''
from ``a period elapsed'', which is why it exists and why \chapref{c16:ch} gates the CRC on it.

Finally, note that \code{consecutive} ends at 4 \textbf{on a run of \code{'0'}s}, and is \textbf{not}
cleared. The next \code{load} inherits both the count and \code{last_bit}, so if that group opens with
a \code{'0'} the run reaches five at its very first bit and a stuff bit is forced before its second.
If it instead opens with a \code{'1'} the run is broken and the count starts over at 1. That is the
same mechanism as the chained reload in the testbench's test 3, where a group instead inherits a run
of four \code{'1'}s and opens with a \code{'1'}.

\section{What the testbench pins down, and putting the module in}
\label{c14:sec:tb}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \code{"11111000"} gives a stuff bit (\code{'0'}) immediately after the fifth \code{'1'}; a
    run of exactly four is \textbf{not} stuffed.
  \item Loading \code{"10101010"} straight after a group that ended with four \code{'1'}s (with no
    reset in between) forces a stuff bit before this group's second real bit, which proves that the
    run state survives \code{load}.
  \item \code{bit_valid} is \code{'1'} at load and at every real-bit or stuff-bit shift, \code{'0'}
    at the closing shift; \code{done} pulses only at that closing shift.
  \item \code{bit_valid} is back at \code{'0'} the cycle \emph{after} every presentation, so a version
    holding the level fails even if it puts out every bit correctly.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Putting it into \code{can_controller}}
\label{c14:sub:wire}

The third block, the same edit. The signal group in \file{controller/can_controller.vhd} is larger
here, since \code{tx_shift_reg} has more to say:

\begin{vhdlcode}
    -- tx_shift_reg.
    signal txsr_load                                          : std_logic;
    signal txsr_data                                          : byte_t;
    signal txsr_bit_count                                     : std_logic_vector(3 downto 0);
    signal txsr_shift                                         : std_logic;
    signal txsr_tx_bit, txsr_stuff, txsr_done, txsr_bit_valid : std_logic;
\end{vhdlcode}

\begin{vhdlcode}
    tx_shift_reg1: entity work.tx_shift_reg
        port map(clock, reset_s2_n, txsr_load, txsr_data, txsr_bit_count, txsr_shift,
                 txsr_tx_bit, txsr_stuff, txsr_done, txsr_bit_valid);
\end{vhdlcode}

One single instance, reused for every stuffed field in every frame. If that still looks surprising,
reread the chapter's introduction: the register is reloaded with a new byte and a new
\code{bit_count} per field, which is exactly what makes one copy enough. (Take the \code{reset_s2_n}
connection as it is for now; \chapref{c17:ch} swaps it for a per-frame reset, for a reason only the
arbitration can show.)
