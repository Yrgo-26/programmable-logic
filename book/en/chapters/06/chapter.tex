\chapter{Counters and shift registers}
\label{c6:ch}

{\large\itshape Building blocks for everything that needs to count or move bits.\par}

\begin{chapterbox}{In this chapter}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Counters: a register, an adder and a constant \code{1}, and why the wraparound comes free.
  \item Shift registers: moving data one bit per clock edge, in a direction used consistently.
  \item SIPO and PISO, and which suits which job.
  \item An 8-bit serial receiver: a shift register plus a bit counter, which knows when a byte has
    arrived.
  \item Two demonstrations on the board: a row of LEDs counting and wrapping, and a byte shifted in
    one button press at a time.
\end{itemize}
\end{chapterbox}

Before the counter reaches \code{1111}: predict what it will show next, and then find the element in
the drawing that makes it happen. There is none, which is the point: nothing in the circuit performs
the wraparound, and it is a consequence of the register's width rather than logic someone put there.

\code{serial_rx8} in \secref{c6:sec:rx8} is the book's first design whose correctness you cannot
check by eye at full speed: continuously enabled, it swallows a byte in 160~ns. That is why it has a
reference testbench, and why the board demonstration in \secref{c6:sec:shiftdemo} drives
\code{shift_enable} from a button instead. Slowing the \emph{clock} down would be the software
instinct and the wrong move; the module already has the control that lets you take one bit at a
time.
