\chapter{Larger networks, multiplexers and submodules}
\label{c2:ch}

{\large\itshape Minimising a network, the first designs that call for a process, and the first
design made of more than one entity.\par}

\begin{chapterbox}{In this chapter}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Karnaugh maps: why a sum of products read straight out of the truth table rarely gives the
    smallest network.
  \item \code{std_logic_vector}: bundling several wires under one name, indexing and slicing.
  \item Multiplexers, from 2-to-1 to 8-to-1.
  \item \code{process} and \code{case}: sequential statements inside otherwise parallel hardware.
  \item Submodules: writing a block once and instantiating it more than once.
  \item Self-checking testbenches, and the three GHDL commands that run one.
\end{itemize}
\end{chapterbox}

\Chapref{c1:ch} took a circuit all the way from a requirement to a file. This chapter does three
things to that path: it tightens the \emph{derivation} with Karnaugh maps, it gives you the VHDL
constructs a larger network needs, and it turns verification into something you run instead of
something you do by eye.

Read \secref{c2:sec:kmap} to \ref{c2:sec:submodules} in order; they build on each other.
\Secref{c2:sec:testbenches} is the book's guide to running testbenches, and every later chapter's
exercises refer back here.

\note[Refresher] The Karnaugh map has a chapter of its own in \debook{}, chapter~3, and the
multiplexer is section~4.2 there.
